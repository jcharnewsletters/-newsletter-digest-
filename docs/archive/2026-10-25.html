The Real Gap Between AI-Generated Apps and Production Systems

  • Building an app and running it are fundamentally different: generating an expense app takes minutes, but running it across 40 countries with tax compliance, permission controls, and reimbursement reconciliation requires substantial human work and business process expertise.
  • Adoption is harder than technology; AI changes who makes which decisions, affecting expertise and professional identity—companies moving fastest gave trusted colleagues access first.
02

Intent-Based Software and the Shift in Developer Work

  • SAP's Joule Work replaces screen-clicking workflows with intent statements and visual workspaces (charts, tables, dashboards) personalized to user needs, eliminating logging into multiple systems to hunt for data.
  • Developer roles shift from implementation to architecture: deciding what's worth building, evaluating business correctness, designing safe agent governance, and handling edge cases.
03

Teaching AI Business Context to Prevent Confident Wrong Answers

  • Enterprise data meaning depends on business processes, unwritten rules, and exceptions—the main AI risk is 'fluent-but-wrong' answers that sound authoritative but miss context.
  • A Knowledge Graph (a machine-readable map of business objects, relationships, and rules) improves AI accuracy and reduces failed API calls; Company Memory captures why past exceptions were approved so the next person doesn't start from scratch.
  • The hardest part is enforcement, not understanding: actions still run with user permissions, defined boundaries, and human approval where required.
04

Cutting AI Costs Without Sacrificing Quality

  • Falling token prices don't cut AI bills because usage spreads; the fix is routing tasks by complexity—simple tasks use smaller models, complex reasoning uses frontier models—plus running models locally and avoiding 'context bloat' (excessive, low-value info in prompts).
  • SAP tested this approach at production scale with 60,000+ internal Joule Work users.
  • For knowledge-intensive industries, faster research and coding increase margin via labor savings; for capital-intensive businesses, better demand forecasts or maintenance decisions matter more than writing speed.
05

Tabular AI for High-Impact Business Predictions

  • Tabular AI (foundation models trained on structured spreadsheet-like data) can predict payment delays and cash flow using historical invoice data with just a few thousand rows.
  • Reducing average collection time from 50 to 45 days at a large company can free tens of millions in working capital.
  • SAP-RPT models offer Explain by Feature (which variables influenced a prediction) and Explain by Example (similar historical records as evidence); future research aims for causal reasoning to recommend effective interventions.
06

Bridging AI Productivity Gains to Actual Profit

  • McKinsey found 80% of AI users report higher productivity, but only 6% of companies see significant profit impact; the gap depends on business model.
  • Labor-intensive industries benefit most from AI writing and coding, while asset- or capital-intensive businesses need better forecasting and logistics decisions instead.

01

Three patterns every vector database should handle

  • Three core AI patterns—RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation; a technique that grounds AI model outputs in your actual data rather than relying only on pre-trained knowledge), agentic memory, and semantic search—each require different vector database (a database that stores and searches data as numerical vectors representing meaning) capabilities.
  • RAG grounds models in your actual data, agentic memory provides persistent cross-session memory for AI agents, and semantic search matches user intent rather than exact keywords.
  • Architecture, not feature checklists, should be the primary criterion for selecting a vector database since each pattern demands different capabilities.
  • Evaluation framework includes four factors: hybrid search (combining keyword-based and vector similarity search), scaling and hosting trade-offs (managed/vendor-operated vs. self-hosted), metadata filtering (filtering by attributes like date or document type alongside semantic matches), and embedding pipeline fit (how well the database integrates with your process for converting raw data into vectors).
  • AWS Marketplace offers an evaluation guide with architecture diagrams and free trials to test vector databases against specific workloads.

01

Rob Snyder's PULL framework for finding product-market fit (PMF)

  • PULL framework (Project, Unavoidable, List of options, Limitations) offers a step-by-step method to find product-market fit (the point where a product satisfies strong market demand), refined by advising 50+ startups to $1M ARR
  • Snyder spent 2 years pushing a product the market didn't want before developing the framework, now documented in his book 'The Power of Pull'
  • Project: start with a specific problem you or someone close to you experiences, not a hypothetical need from research
  • Unavoidable: confirm the problem is painful enough that people actively avoid it or suffer real consequences if unsolved
  • List of options: map all existing ways people currently solve or work around the problem to understand the competitive landscape
  • Limitations: pinpoint where current solutions fall short, revealing the opening for a better solution
  • Ideas rooted in a founder's own unavoidable pain carry stronger conviction and insight than externally researched opportunities
  • Founders who lived the problem can spot competitive gaps and solution failures that others miss
  • Cold outreach and first sales calls should lead with the customer's problem and current solution, not the product title pitch
  • First calls should be discovery conversations to validate shared pain, not sales closes; avoid overly artificial personalization
  • Design partners (early users who help shape a product pre-launch) often give falsely positive feedback because they're emotionally invested and face no real cost if the solution fails
  • True validation comes from cold, paying customers rather than hand-selected design partners who bias results toward approval
  • The PULL framework also applies to larger companies seeking new growth opportunities using the same validation approach
