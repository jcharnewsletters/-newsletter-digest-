Bitcoin Trades Far Below JPMorgan's Mining Cost Estimate

Claude/Anthropic Researcher Claims Proof of the Riemann Hypothesis

  • Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge used Claude to produce a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis (a 167-year-old unsolved problem from 1859 asking whether prime numbers follow a predictable pattern) after about 6 weeks of work.
  • The proof spans 17 million lines of machine-verifiable code and was certified correct by the Lean proof checker (automated software that verifies mathematical proofs line-by-line) and the Mathlib community.
  • The Riemann Hypothesis carries a $1 million Millennium Prize from the Clay Institute, established in 2000.
  • Alpöge said Claude 'far outpaced' him in the final stretch of the work, and critics (including the Association for Human Mathematics) called the proof 'little more than publicity,' arguing no human can reasonably verify 17 million lines of code.
  • A rival proof from Alessandro Masullo, reportedly produced without AI over 12 years, surfaced hours later but reviewers quickly flagged errors in it; top mathematicians had expected this problem to take decades more to solve.

Superhuman (Zain Kahn) Alpöge suggested more AI-driven mathematical 'miracles' are ahead, feeding into broader superintelligence timeline claims from industry figures like Meta's AI chief.

OpenAI's Proposed Shared AI Capability Limits via Perplexity Metric

  • OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki proposed that frontier AI labs agree on shared capability thresholds measured by perplexity/log-perplexity (a measure of how well a model predicts the next word in text; lower scores mean better predictions), and published OpenAI's own numbers: GPT-3.5 at 0.99, GPT-4 at 0.91.
  • Scores below 0.8 correlate with emerging transformative abilities in cybersecurity and mathematics; around 0.76 is when OpenAI's models began producing results on open math problems.
  • An unreleased internal OpenAI model reportedly sits at about 0.73, with Pachocki predicting 'transformative capabilities' in the 0.68–0.7 range.
  • Sam Altman called the post 'the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while' and recommended rival labs agree on shared limits until AI monitoring tools catch up with capabilities.
  • The metric has notable gaps: different labs use different datasets making cross-lab comparisons difficult, perplexity doesn't map cleanly to specific real-world tasks, and chain-of-thought reasoning improvements (letting a model show step-by-step reasoning) don't necessarily lower perplexity scores.

The Neuron Success of the proposal depends on whether other labs publish their own perplexity numbers and on concrete 'understanding' milestones, such as successor tools to chain-of-thought monitors that catch problems in model reasoning.

Coding Agents Struggle with Real-World Task Completion

  • SWE-CoWork tested 11 models on 101 workplace-style assignments with conflicting requirements scattered across simulated colleagues; top models passed only 63% of key fail-to-pass tests but completed fewer than 8% of full assignments.
  • 31% of requirements agents reported as finished actually failed verification tests, exposing a gap between partially correct work and fully completed tasks.
