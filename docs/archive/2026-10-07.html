Polymarket Launches Protocol V2: Ground-Up Rebuild

  • Polymarket rebuilt its entire prediction market system (a platform where users bet on outcomes of real-world events) from scratch, replacing the 2019 Gnosis Conditional Tokens Framework.
  • New architecture consolidates fragmented contract layers into a single ERC1155 positions contract, one pUSD collateral token, one exchange, and one router.
  • Settlement (the mechanism determining winners and paying out profits) is now faster, reducing wait times for payouts after events resolve.
  • An OracleAggregator with pluggable resolution modules connects to UMA, Chainlink, and future oracle sources (services that feed real-world data on-chain) for flexible market resolution.
  • First-class multi-chain bridging support for positions, collateral, and resolutions lays groundwork for expansion without disrupting existing integrations.
  • Launches with four market types — Binary, Atomic Neg-risk, Incremental Neg-risk, and Combinatorial — upgradeable via governance.
  • Rebuild targets higher concurrent trading volume and lower transaction costs, addressing technical debt from years on the old system.

TLDR Crypto Specifies the new protocol replaces the 2019 Gnosis Conditional Tokens Framework and details the exact consolidated contract structure and four launch market types.

OpenAI releases 722 math manuscripts from internal model

  • An internal, unreleased OpenAI model produced 722 manuscripts (grouped into 372 result families) tackling roughly 4,000 open problems in pure mathematics, theoretical computer science, and mathematical physics.
  • Boldest claim is a proof of the quasi-Riemann hypothesis, a weaker cousin of the famous $1M Riemann Hypothesis about how prime numbers are distributed.
  • Most results took an average of just 3 hours of ChatGPT Pro compute (extended reasoning mode) per result, far less than last month's Navier-Stokes proof which needed 10,000 AI agents.
  • Many papers include Lean formalizations (software that mechanically verifies every logical step of a proof), though not all results have this, so some unformalized claims could still contain errors.
  • Reaction is split: Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge called it 'the most significant moment in mathematical history,' while other mathematicians are angry OpenAI broke its promise to space out releases, and 28 mathematicians signed a September open letter opposing AI labs' use of unpublished research without credit.
  • The real bottleneck now is verification and absorption — how fast human mathematicians can check and build on the work — rather than how many results can be generated.

01

Why Standard Benchmarks Don't Work

  • Published benchmarks (standardized tests comparing AI models) leak into model training data and lose validity over time; the real question is fit for your workflow, not abstract superiority.
  • Frontier models release every 11 days on average; meaningful verdicts emerge only after 7 days when people do actual work, not toy demos like 3D games.
  • Even in locked-down enterprises, choices remain: model tier selection, inference effort levels, and tool combinations all deserve testing against your real tasks.
02

The Five-Step Personal Benchmark System

  • Step 1—Choose 4–6 tasks mirroring your actual work balance, done regularly, trusted enough to change habits over; include at least one previous AI failure case and one where bad output is easy to spot.
  • Step 2—Compare current setup against 1–4 new candidates (models, harnesses, or tool combinations); baseline always included to avoid pure novelty bias.
  • Step 3—Run identical prompts in fresh chat sessions on each model; use manual testing, API scripts, or team platforms like LangSmith and Langfuse; run high-stakes tests multiple times since identical inputs produce different outputs; keep company data out of third-party routers.
  • Step 4—Blind-score anonymized outputs 1–5 with notes; side-by-side comparison beats absolute scoring; use randomization via spreadsheet, friend, or script to eliminate tool preference bias.
  • Step 5—Winning one test doesn't guarantee switching; weigh cost, latency, terms, features, and habit disruption against performance gains before deciding.
03

Actual Test Results: Seven Models Across Six Tasks

  • No single winner: Kimi K3 led on cost and speed; GPT 6.1 Sol dominated pricing negotiation at 1/10th the cost of alternatives; Opus 5.5 built the best course website; Grok 4.7 was significantly slowest.
  • Human and AI judge (Gemini Pro) agreed only on the pricing negotiation task; the judge scored websites from code output rather than visuals, revealing judge limitations—human blind scoring proved more reliable.
  • Kimi, Fable 5.1, and Opus 5 tied on LinkedIn post writing; GPT models won the course exercise; routine tasks now achievable on open-weight models (publicly downloadable AI models) without premium subscriptions.
  • Cost per task matters more than monthly subscription rates; $200/month plans subsidize actual token consumption, so per-use pricing wins for consumption-based users or those hitting rate limits.
04

Implementation Guidance

  • Match inference effort (speed/quality tradeoff) settings across models or accept OpenRouter defaults and test effort levels as a separate variable.
  • Operations tasks needing connectors and persistent context must run in actual harnesses (software wrappers that give a model tools and memory), not bare APIs, to reveal real-world performance.
  • Table-stakes work like email triage and background research: open models from Kimi, Mistral, and GLM are production-ready; explicit constraints beat vague prompts for measurable evaluation.
