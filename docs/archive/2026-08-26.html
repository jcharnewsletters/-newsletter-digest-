Frozen Knowledge vs. Living Context: Why AI Needs Both

  • AI models arrive frozen with a knowledge cutoff (the date after which the model has no training data), requiring external context to stay relevant and personalized.
  • Static context covers slow-changing facts like formatting preferences and job title; set once in ChatGPT or Claude's system instructions and update quarterly when situations change.
  • Living context pulls frequently updated information from email, calendar, and web search; connect AI models to actual data sources instead of stale memories.
  • Both ChatGPT and Claude automatically save memories about you (preferences, work details), but these are frozen knowledge, not living context—review and edit them in Settings > Memory to stay accurate.
  • Rule of thumb: information that rarely changes needs static context (set and forget); information that changes daily/weekly needs living context (connect to a live source).
  • Modern AI models are pre-trained on massive text snapshots to predict the next word, then post-trained on examples of helpful answers, but this training is frozen after the model ships.
  • ChatGPT (Settings > Personalization > Custom Instructions) and Claude (Settings > Custom Instructions) both support standing personal instructions plus Projects with uploaded reference documents.
  • Better context input leads to better personalized results regardless of how smart the underlying model becomes, since even frontier models start each conversation knowing nothing about you.

The Real Problem Is Lazy AI Writing, Not AI Writing Itself

  • The op-ed contained obvious AI hallmarks ('it's-not-this-it's-that' phrasing, jargon clusters) fixable with minimal editing; failure to remove them signaled the author didn't bother tuning the output.
  • Perceived low effort in writing undermines the argument itself—if the author wouldn't invest time to fix the prose, readers doubt they invested thought in the substance.
  • Proposed core principle: stop asking whether AI wrote something; ask whether the output quality matches the importance of what's being communicated.
03

Five Rules for Using AI in Writing

  • Different writing types need different standards: emails need no prose polish, but persuasive op-eds must show obvious effort or readers dismiss the argument.
  • Purity tests about AI use are fading, but quality standards will stay high—using AI for writing will feel as normal as GPS, but bad writing won't become acceptable.
  • AI works best in the middle of the process (research, organization, drafting, iteration), with humans needed at the start (deciding what to argue) and end (verifying it's true and worth saying).
  • Shorter output is usually better; early AI models excelled at volume over concision, producing bloated, tiring documents.
  • Writing is thinking—outsourcing prose without doing the intellectual work yourself risks slowly eroding your own thinking, since the cognitive cost accumulates over time, unlike simple tool use.
04

Where AI Writing Works Best in Practice

  • Email and meeting notes are safe uses—AI compresses transcripts better than humans, though humans should add the one or two insights that mattered.
  • Internal strategy memos are deceptively risky since strategy is where writing becomes thinking; best practice is to lock a tight outline yourself, then let AI draft around it.
  • Marketing copy remains persistently weak from AI due to generic sameness; better used for rapid iteration (e.g., generating 20 taglines) than final copy.
  • Op-eds and persuasive essays face the highest bar, since readers instantly detect low effort and disengage.
