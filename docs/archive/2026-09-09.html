OpenAI's Internal Model Solves $1M Navier-Stokes Problem Amid Plagiarism Controversy

  • OpenAI's unreleased internal model (more capable than the just-released GPT-6 Astra) solved Navier-Stokes—one of seven $1M Millennium Prize Problems, concerning whether fluid flow equations (fundamental to engineering jet engines and modeling blood flow) can produce infinite velocity—using roughly 10,000 parallel AI agents (workers running simultaneously on the same task) over 88 hours.
  • The effort generated 2.7M messages and 130B output tokens, at an estimated compute cost of 'millions of dollars'; GPT-6 Astra then formalized the proof in Lean (a proof-checking language that verifies mathematical logic step by step).
  • Sam Altman called it 'one of the most amazing moments' in OpenAI history.
  • NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster and Anthropic's Levent Alpöge had spent about a year on a similar proof path and posted partial results the night before OpenAI's announcement, alleging OpenAI may have accessed their work via Codex (OpenAI's code-generation model) drafts they had uploaded.
  • OpenAI, including researcher Sébastien Bubeck and Sam Altman, denies the allegations, stating it 'did not see any of their work' and 'no specific user data was accessed'—though it acknowledged usage data may have improved its models, a non-denial that has fueled controversy.
  • The dispute has broader implications: academics worry AI labs may exploit private uploaded research and absorb ideas without permission.

The Neuron Key framing shift proposed: judge frontier AI by what a system accomplishes with enough agents, parallel compute, and tools—not by single-turn chatbot responses.

The Rundown AI OpenAI claims the model responsible is already more capable than GPT-6 Astra, its most recently released model.
