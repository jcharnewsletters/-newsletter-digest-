How text watermarking detects AI-generated content

  • Language models pick tokens (words or word pieces) one at a time; watermarking subtly biases certain 'green-list' tokens to be slightly more likely, without changing readable quality.
  • A secret key splits the vocabulary into green and red tokens at each step, with the split constantly changing so readers can't spot a pattern.
  • Detection works by having the secret-key holder count green tokens and apply a z-score statistical test to see if the count exceeds random chance.
  • Implementation (e.g., via Hugging Face Transformers) uses a green-list ratio (like 0.25, meaning 25% of vocabulary) and a bias strength (like 2.5) to balance detectability against writing quality, and can run on a laptop CPU.
02

Watermarking's limits and regulatory push

  • Watermarks survive light edits but are destroyed by heavy paraphrasing or translation, since that swaps out the original watermarked tokens; Google's SynthID Text has similar limits.
  • Watermarking can't be added retroactively—it only works for models that implement it during generation, and proves nothing about unwatermarked text.
  • A watermark flag alone should not be treated as proof of AI authorship for consequences like academic discipline.
  • The EU AI Act requires generative AI providers to mark outputs in machine-readable ways, with compliance required by December 2, 2026, driving adoption of such techniques.
  • Context: current AI-detection tools remain unreliable, often flagging human-written work as AI-generated, which is pushing the shift toward watermarking as a more rigorous alternative.
